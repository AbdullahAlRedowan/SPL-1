The history of the war and military service during the century was recorded 
by an alert adjutant sitting in the orderly room. In that place of debate, 
tumult, and writhing writs, he sat unfevered mid the storm, telling everyone 
what was what. The young soldiers, conceived between battles and raised amid 
the harsh noises of war, marched forward under heavy command across the desert 
sand towards the distant wells.

While France presented her youth as a tax to Caesar, the staying at home 
members of parliament in Westminster debated military estimates, tonnage, 
and the disastrous policy affecting trade and industrial life. Mr. Pringle 
and Captain Tryon delivered urgent speeches on the House floor concerning 
Belgium's guaranteed neutrality and the European conflict. Meanwhile, on the 
frontline, little Charles recovered at the Invalides, earning the Croix de Guerre 
and Medaille Militaire with palm for his immense courage, returning salutes from 
passing officers along the Boulevards with crimson blushes.

The poet, born of noble parentage in Paris, abandoned his early romantic 
daring for subjective strains of desolate emptiness and lost faith. Yet, 
his genial, ironical temperament and undying achievements remained classic 
for all time. Just like the general's mythical desert expeditions involving 
transport wagons, old Arab shepherds, and young camels near the palm trees, 
the poet's lyre sang of tender passion, ethical disintegration, and the 
agonizing moral malady of an ardent, pale generation.

In the end, whether in dream reviews, air-raid warnings, or solemn court 
sessions, the unheeded warning of youth left its mark. The court is shaking in 
every shoe, as the bishops shout at the gate, while soldiers roll themselves 
in camp-fire blankets, wondering about porridge, sausages, and the noble task 
of duty to the nation.
